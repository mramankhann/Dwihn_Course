# Module 9 · AI Governance & Incident Handling

**Month 2: Advanced AI & Lumenore Mastery · Week 8 · [Session 11](/sessions/s11-governance)**

## Topic introduction

Governance gives staff a reliable place to resolve permission questions and report problems. The handbook names an AI Governance Committee, a 24-hour response theme, a five-step incident exercise, and manager responsibilities. It does **not** supply the DWIHN committee roster, internal reporting contact, or exact incident procedure. The instructor must replace training examples with the current official process before live use.

**By the end:** route a new AI use case to the right owner, recognize a possible incident, practice a safe response, and describe how managers support reporting.

## Point 1 · Understand the AI Governance Committee's role

An AI governance function should clarify who approves tools and use cases, who defines data and privacy rules, who owns security review, who trains staff, and who learns from incidents. Individual employees do not need to solve every policy question themselves. They need a clear route for questions and a record of the approved answer. Until a new use case has been reviewed, do not treat silence or another team's practice as permission.

**Example:** A staff member wants a new AI feature to summarize operational notes. The catalog does not mention it. Their manager routes the use case, data type, benefit, and proposed review controls to the designated governance owner instead of guessing.

**Apply it:** Record the current DWIHN contact or channel for **tool approval, privacy, security, data ownership, and training**. Mark any missing contact for instructor follow-up.

Governance is the everyday route by which an organization answers questions before they turn into inconsistent practice. A useful structure has clear owners for policy, tool review, privacy and security assessment, data stewardship, workforce training, and ongoing oversight. The source handbook names an AI Governance Committee, but it does not provide a roster or describe its authority. Instructors should not invent committee members or say that a particular group approves a use case unless DWIHN confirms it.

When a staff member proposes a new workflow, the review should start with the task rather than a product demonstration. What problem is the team trying to solve? Which people and data are involved? What tool or feature is proposed? What could go wrong? How would a human review the result? What happens if the system is unavailable or inaccurate? These details help the right owner assess benefit and risk together.

Staff can contribute to governance by asking clear questions, using approved channels, and sharing recurring concerns. Governance should make the safe path understandable and practical. If the only response to uncertainty is “be careful,” people will fill the gap differently. A specific reference, owner, and expected response process build more reliable habits.

## Point 2 · Recognize and report a possible incident promptly

A possible AI incident may involve sensitive information in an unapproved environment, sharing an output with the wrong recipient, an inaccurate AI result used in work, or unexpected system behavior. Staff should report a **suspected** incident so the appropriate team can assess it; they do not have to prove harm first. The handbook and proposal refer to responding within 24 hours, but this is a course theme, **not a universal HIPAA legal deadline or permission to wait**. Follow the current DWIHN internal rule and report as soon as the issue is recognized.

**Example:** A worker realizes that a copied prompt may have contained member information. They stop further use, note the tool, account, time, and content involved, and contact the official incident channel promptly.

**Apply it:** Keep the [incident card](/resources/incident-card) visible. Do not discuss sensitive details in an unapproved support chat.

The handbook’s “within 24 hours” language is a proposed training target, not an instruction to wait a day before telling anyone and not a universal HIPAA notification deadline. HHS notification requirements apply to covered entities and business associates under defined circumstances; organizational responders assess the facts and determine the applicable requirements. A frontline employee’s job is to recognize and report a possible event promptly using the current internal channel. [HHS Breach Notification Rule](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/breach-notification/index.html).

Examples of possible incidents include entering sensitive information in an unapproved tool, sharing a generated report with the wrong group, discovering that an AI summary materially misstates a record, or noticing unexpected access or system behavior. Not every error has the same impact, and employees should not decide on their own that something is harmless. Early reporting gives the organization a chance to assess access, contain further exposure, correct affected work, and meet its own procedures.

Give a factual account. State what information category may be involved, which system or feature was used, when the event occurred, where the output may have gone, and what action has already been taken. Clearly label unknowns. Avoid speculation about who saw the material or whether a legal breach occurred. Do not forward sensitive content to a larger audience as proof of the problem.

## Point 3 · Practice a five-step response

The handbook offers a **training sequence**, because the source does not specify DWIHN's exact five steps: **recognize**, **stop or contain further exposure where safe**, **preserve relevant facts**, **report through the designated channel**, and **cooperate with follow-up**. Do not investigate beyond your role, silently delete records, contact external parties on your own, or make a legal determination. The official privacy and security procedure always takes precedence.

**Example:** In a tabletop exercise, a fictional staff member uploads a sensitive file to a personal AI account. The learner identifies the exposure, stops repeating it, records what happened, follows the approved internal reporting route, and awaits instructions for any further action.

**Apply it:** Time the first response in a simulation and practice stating the facts without speculating about the outcome. See [S11](/sessions/s11-governance).

The five actions are a teaching sequence that helps staff act calmly: notice the event, prevent further exposure if safe and within your role, keep relevant facts, report, and cooperate with the response team. “Contain” does not mean improvising technical cleanup. For example, a worker can stop sending the file and avoid reopening the workflow, but should follow instructions before deleting a message, changing access, or contacting a vendor. Some actions can destroy evidence or complicate assessment.

Practice the report out loud in plain language: “At approximately 10:15, I used the personal account to ask for a summary. I may have pasted text from a restricted record. I stopped the task and have not shared the answer. I am contacting the internal response channel now. I do not know whether the input was retained.” This is more useful than a vague apology or a confident guess that no one saw it.

After reporting, follow directions from the designated incident lead. They may ask for more details, preserve records, or avoid further communication. The employee should not investigate other users, test the system with additional sensitive data, or message members or external parties unless specifically instructed through the official process.

## Point 4 · Managers make safe use discussable

Managers reinforce the approved-tool rule, make time for staff to learn classification, and respond constructively when people raise uncertainty or report a mistake. They should identify a reviewer for new workflows, ensure the team knows where current guidance lives, and prevent productivity pressure from becoming a reason to bypass checks. They also route repeated questions to the policy owner and share the authoritative answer with the team.

**Example:** A team member asks whether AI may process a new report. The manager helps define the task and data, checks the catalog, and requests governance guidance if no entry fits. The manager does not say “try it and see.”

**Apply it:** Build a team check-in that asks what AI tasks are being attempted, which rule is unclear, and whether any output or sharing problem needs review.

Managers shape the conditions in which staff make decisions. If leaders ask for speed without allowing review time, employees may feel pushed to skip safety checks. If questions are treated as resistance, people may stop raising uncertainties until something goes wrong. A supportive manager can set reasonable boundaries: use the catalog, ask before moving uncertain data, and keep a human accountable for the result. This makes safe practice part of ordinary work rather than a personal preference.

Managers should know where to find current policy, who owns tool approval, and how to start an incident report. They do not need to become privacy or technical specialists. Their job is often to route the question, protect time for training, make sure employees can reach the right owner, and share the answer once it is confirmed. Where an employee has reported a possible incident, the manager should support prompt reporting and avoid blame or informal investigation.

Teams can learn from near misses by asking what made the unsafe action easy: a missing catalog entry, confusing labels, unclear permissions, a rushed deadline, or a job aid that was hard to find. Governance can then improve the workflow. A learning culture does not excuse careless handling; it helps correct the conditions that produce repeated mistakes.

## Key takeaways

- Governance provides an approval and question path before a new use case begins.
- Report suspected incidents promptly under the current internal procedure.
- The five-step exercise is a learning aid; official DWIHN instructions control real response.
- Managers should make questions and early reporting routine.

## Practical scenario and exercise

**Scenario:** A fictional staff member believes sensitive text may have entered an unapproved AI environment.

1. State what makes this a possible incident and what immediate activity should stop.
2. List facts to preserve: tool/account, time, content category, recipient or sharing path, and actions already taken.
3. Identify the official internal contact or mark it as a required instructor confirmation.
4. Walk through the five training steps without taking actions reserved for incident responders.
5. Describe how a manager should support the staff member and prevent recurrence.

**Worked walkthrough:** A staff member realizes that text from a synthetic practice exercise may have included an actual identifier before being entered into a personal account. The employee stops using the account for the task, avoids sharing the generated output, and writes down when it happened, which account was used, the type of information, and any sharing that occurred. They contact the designated internal channel promptly and say which details are still unknown. Their manager supports the report and helps the employee follow instructions from the response lead. The employee does not try to test whether the text was retained or decide that no report is needed. The incident team assesses the event and directs follow-up.

**Knowledge check:** Must harm be proven before reporting? Does “within 24 hours” justify waiting? Who can approve a new AI workflow?

**Answer cues:** No proof of harm is required before reporting a concern. The 24-hour phrase is not permission to delay; use the current internal timing rule and report as soon as recognized. Only the designated DWIHN owner or governance process can approve a new use. Strong answers clearly separate a staff member’s factual report from the organization’s assessment.

**Role transfer:** Managers can use a short tabletop during a team meeting without asking anyone to disclose a real incident. Give the group a synthetic event, ask what the first report should contain, and confirm that every person knows the current contact. If the contact, backup route, or response instruction is unknown, assign the program owner to supply it before staff use the affected workflow. Keep the job aid aligned with the official procedure as it changes.

**Facilitator discussion:** In the tabletop, separate the staff member’s immediate responsibilities from the incident lead’s work. Have participants identify which facts they can report and which conclusions they should leave open. End by naming the current internal channel and a backup route; if either is unknown, record it as a pre-delivery action for the program owner.
