# Module 2 · Approved Tools & HIPAA Safety

**Month 1: AI Foundations & Safe Usage · Week 2 · [Session 2](/sessions/s02-tools-and-data)**

## Topic introduction

Workplace AI safety depends on the **specific tool, account, feature, task, and data**. A well-known product is not automatically approved for DWIHN work. This module teaches the decision path for finding the current Approved AI Tool Catalog, protecting health information, recognizing the four course data labels, and responding when something goes wrong. The supplied handbook does not contain the live catalog or official tier definitions; the local policy owner must supply them for real use.

**By the end:** explain why catalog approval is specific, describe HIPAA-safe habits, make a provisional tier choice, and identify a safe stop/report decision.

## Point 1 · Use the DWIHN Approved AI Tool Catalog

Start with the current catalog entry for the intended workflow. Check the exact tenant or account, enabled feature, authorized role, allowed purpose and information types, and rules for uploads, recording, retention, sharing, and export. Approval of one feature does not carry over to a different feature or a personal account. If an entry is missing or ambiguous, ask the designated owner before testing with real information.

**Example:** An employee wants to summarize an internal document with a familiar AI website. They first determine the document's tier and look for a catalog entry approving that website, account, and task. A missing entry leads to a pause, not a trial upload.

**Apply it:** Record the catalog version or link, the relevant restriction, and the person who can resolve an unanswered question. The instructor should demonstrate with the **actual current catalog** or a clearly labeled sample.

Think of approval as a description of a particular **workflow**, not a gold star attached to a product. A workflow includes who signs in, which feature is used, what information enters it, what the vendor or system does with that information, where the result is stored, and who can see or export it. A product may offer several features with different settings. A staff member may also have access to an account that is intended for personal use rather than organizational work. The catalog should make those distinctions clear enough to check before a task begins.

During a catalog review, read the conditions as carefully as the tool name. Look for permitted data categories, role limits, training versus production settings, external sharing, recording, retention, and whether the workflow is limited to a particular department. Ask what happens to an input after the task: is it retained, used to improve a service, copied into a record, or available to administrators? Those are questions for the approved catalog and system owner; a public product page may not answer them for DWIHN’s configuration.

If there is no catalog entry, write a short use-case description for the owner: task and benefit, data involved, proposed feature, users, output destination, and human review. This gives governance or IT a concrete question to assess. Do not substitute “my colleague used it” or “the vendor says it is secure” for the organization’s approval.

## Point 2 · HIPAA boundaries are daily behavior

Protected health information needs appropriate safeguards in each system that receives, stores, or processes it. HIPAA considerations include authorized use, access controls, vendor arrangements, and careful disclosure. For many uses and disclosures, the minimum necessary standard asks that the information used be limited to what is needed; its exceptions and DWIHN's specific workflow guidance matter. Removing a name alone may leave enough detail to identify someone. Follow the current DWIHN privacy process rather than assuming a tool's marketing claim permits PHI. See [HHS cloud guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html) and [minimum necessary guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/minimum-necessary-requirement/index.html).

**Example:** A member communication needs a clearer tone. A worker does not paste the complete record into an unconfirmed tool. They check the permitted workflow and use only the information authorized for that task.

**Apply it:** Check both the **input** and the **output destination**. A safe draft can become an unsafe disclosure if copied into the wrong channel.

HIPAA is a set of rules for covered entities and business associates, not a certification badge for a single chatbot. HHS guidance says that a cloud service provider creating, receiving, maintaining, or transmitting ePHI on behalf of a regulated organization generally has business-associate obligations, and the organization must address the applicable agreement and safeguards. Encryption or a product’s marketing description alone does not establish that a particular DWIHN workflow is permitted. The organization’s risk analysis and approved configuration still matter. See [HHS guidance on HIPAA and cloud computing](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html).

The minimum-necessary standard is also a useful work habit, but learners should not treat it as a universal instruction to remove information from every task. HHS describes a general requirement to take reasonable steps to limit many uses, disclosures, and requests to what is needed for the purpose, with defined exceptions. Treatment disclosures and other specific situations can be handled differently. Follow the applicable DWIHN direction and ask privacy staff when unsure; this course is not a substitute for a policy decision. See [HHS minimum-necessary guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/minimum-necessary-requirement/index.html).

In practice, ask three separate questions: **Am I allowed to access this information? Is this system approved to process it for this task? Is the output allowed to go to this recipient or record?** Permission to see a record does not automatically authorize copying it into another system. A permitted input can also become a disclosure problem if the finished draft is pasted into a broad channel or an unapproved document store.

## Point 3 · The four data classification tiers

The handbook uses **Public, Internal, Confidential, and Restricted** as a progression of protection. A published service notice may be Public; a nonpublic operational agenda may be Internal; a staff or contract matter may be Confidential; identifiable member health information may require Restricted handling. These are **provisional teaching examples**. The exact DWIHN definitions and allowed destinations must come from its official classification policy. Mixed files require attention to the most sensitive relevant element.

**Example:** A public event flyer and a member-specific care note are both text, but their handling decisions differ sharply. The first may be suitable for a public wording exercise after checking it is published; the second requires an explicitly authorized workflow and role.

**Apply it:** Use the [data classification desk card](/resources/data-desk-card), then confirm the real tier and tool rule before any upload.

Classification and tool selection answer different questions. Classification describes the care the information needs; the catalog tells you which systems and uses are permitted under specified conditions. A label by itself does not authorize a task, and a tool entry does not reclassify the information. A report may contain several tiers at once. If policy allows the sensitive section to be separated, use the approved process and confirm that the remaining material cannot identify someone through combinations such as a rare event, date, location, or small group.

The course uses the four labels to rehearse decisions, but only the current DWIHN policy can determine whether a particular staffing table is Internal or Confidential, or whether a ZIP-level result needs additional disclosure review. Learners should practice saying, “Here is my provisional classification; here is the policy question I need answered.” That is a stronger workplace response than presenting an uncertain label as a fact.

When training with a sample catalog, the instructor should point out which entries are invented for the exercise. Ask learners to locate the scope and restrictions, not just the product name. Then remove the sample and have them explain what they would do if the official catalog were unavailable: pause, protect the information, and contact the designated owner.

## Point 4 · Prevent and report mistakes

Unsafe AI use can create privacy exposure, incorrect communications, compliance problems, and loss of trust. Prevention is a repeatable sequence: classify, confirm the tool, limit the data, review the answer, and check recipients. If information may have gone to the wrong place, stop further sharing and report promptly using the current DWIHN process. Do not quietly delete evidence or wait to see whether harm occurs; the responsible team needs facts to assess the event.

**Example:** An employee notices after submission that text may have included sensitive member details in an unapproved environment. They stop using that workflow, note what was submitted and when, and contact the designated internal response channel according to policy.

**Apply it:** Keep the [incident card](/resources/incident-card) available, and ask the instructor to identify the current contact before live training.

An incident report is a request for the organization to assess what happened; it is not a personal finding of fault or a legal conclusion. Staff should report what they know, separate that from what they suspect, and avoid trying to determine on their own whether a reportable breach occurred. HHS describes breach assessment and notification obligations for covered entities and business associates, while DWIHN’s response team decides what applies to a specific event. [HHS Breach Notification Rule](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/breach-notification/index.html).

Useful facts may include the approximate time, system or account, type of information, intended and actual recipients, whether a file or link was shared, and what steps were already taken. Do not copy sensitive text into a second system to demonstrate the mistake. Preserve relevant details according to instructions and let the response team decide what evidence is needed. If you realize a mistake immediately, stop further sharing and report promptly through the approved channel rather than waiting to see if someone notices.

Prevention is stronger when teams can discuss near misses. A staff member who asks before pasting an uncertain document has surfaced a useful control gap. Managers should respond by checking the rule and improving access to guidance, not by encouraging staff to “just try it.”

## Key takeaways

- A product name or personal account does not establish workplace approval.
- Classify the data and confirm the exact permitted workflow before entering it.
- Minimize information and check both output accuracy and sharing destination.
- Report a suspected mistake early through the official process.

## Practical scenario and exercise

**Scenario:** A colleague proposes using a familiar public AI website to rewrite a member-facing message. The draft text may include identifying health information.

1. Name the objective and the sensitive elements you would look for.
2. Identify the provisional tier and the official DWIHN definition you must verify.
3. List the catalog fields needed to approve the exact workflow. If any are unknown, state the stop decision.
4. Offer a safe way to make progress without entering the text into an unapproved tool.
5. Describe the first report action if the text had already been submitted.

**Worked walkthrough:** A worker has a draft message with a member’s name, appointment date, and a rare service detail. The worker’s objective is to make the message easier to understand. The first question is not which chatbot writes best; it is whether the proposed system and account are approved for this exact data and task. If the catalog does not say, the worker stops and asks the designated owner. They may still progress by working from a public template or a synthetic example while waiting. If the text has already been submitted, the worker stops further use, records the basic facts without copying the message into another chat, and reports promptly through the current internal channel. The incident team, not the worker, determines what assessment or notification is required.

**Knowledge check:** Does “approved tool” mean every feature is approved? Why is removing a name insufficient proof of de-identification? What should happen if the catalog has no answer?

**Answer cues:** Product approval is specific to the account, feature, purpose, role, data, and conditions. Other details may identify a person, especially in combination. If the catalog is silent or unclear, pause and contact the authorized owner. A strong response separates the employee’s immediate action from the incident team’s later assessment.

**Facilitator note:** Do not use a live member message for the demonstration. Have participants identify the task, role, data category, tool entry, output location, and unresolved policy question on the same worksheet. Score the reasoning, not whether they guessed a local answer that was never supplied.
