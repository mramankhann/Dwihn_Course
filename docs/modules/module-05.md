# Module 5 · AI in Your Daily DWIHN Work

**Month 1: AI Foundations & Safe Usage · Week 3 · [Session 4](/sessions/s04-daily-work)**

## Topic introduction

AI use becomes valuable when it fits an actual role and ends in a reviewed work product. A coordinator, manager, administrator, and compliance reviewer may each have different data permissions and standards for final approval. This module turns the safety and prompting skills from Modules 1–4 into a repeatable workflow.

**By the end:** choose an appropriate role task, draft an email or summary, edit AI output against a source, and use the daily checklist.

## Point 1 · Design role-specific workflows

Start with the job to be done and the responsible employee. A coordinator might organize **approved** notes into follow-up items. A program manager might draft a narrative from a validated aggregate chart. An administrator might simplify an internal announcement. A compliance reviewer might use AI to list review questions while retaining authority for policy interpretation. Each workflow must specify data classification, catalog approval, input boundary, human reviewer, and final destination. Do not copy a colleague's workflow just because their tool access differs.

**Example:** A coordinator wants to convert synthetic meeting notes into an action list. The prompt asks for owner, due date, and unresolved items. The coordinator checks each against the notes and sends only the corrected list through the authorized channel.

**Apply it:** Write a five-step workflow for one recurring task in your own role, naming a **stop condition** when the source or permission is unclear.

Map the work before choosing the AI step. A useful workflow begins with a request or recurring need, identifies the information and its owner, checks the approved system, and defines the human review. Only then should the tool be asked to draft, sort, or summarize. The finished output returns to the normal work process; it should not create a shadow record that staff forget to reconcile. This sequence makes it easier to see where automation helps and where it could interrupt an existing control.

For a coordinator, an appropriate training task might be converting a fictional meeting transcript into a list of explicitly assigned follow-ups. For an operations manager, it might be outlining a weekly update from validated aggregate figures. An administrator could reorganize a public announcement while preserving its dates and contact information. A compliance employee might group policy questions for a meeting but still rely on approved sources for interpretation. These examples show different kinds of work; they do not establish that live member, staff, or compliance material may be entered into any named tool.

The stop condition deserves as much attention as the happy path. It might be “pause if the catalog does not cover this data type,” “ask the metric owner if the denominator is unclear,” or “do not send if the draft changes a commitment.” A workflow that says what to do when something goes wrong is easier to use responsibly than one that assumes everything will work on the first try.

## Point 2 · Draft emails, summaries, and reports

AI can save blank-page time, but each document has a different test. An **email** needs a correct audience, action request, and tone. A **summary** must preserve decisions, caveats, and follow-ups rather than merely shorten text. A **report** must distinguish verified numbers from interpretation and proposed action. Provide only permitted context and give the tool a structure that makes review simple.

**Example:** A worker asks for a concise internal update from synthetic bullets. The output says “the team completed the referral redesign,” while the notes say “the team discussed a redesign.” The sender corrects the status, checks the meeting date, and removes a commitment no one approved.

**Apply it:** Annotate the draft with **source fact**, **AI wording**, **human edit**, and **final reviewer**. Keep an audit trail for high-impact communications according to local procedure.

Different formats carry different risks. In an email, a small tone change can sound like a promise or a reprimand. In meeting notes, changing “discussed” to “approved” alters the record of a decision. In a report, moving a percentage without its denominator can exaggerate a result. In a summary, a missing exception may be more important than several accurate general statements. Tell the tool what the document is for, who will read it, and which details must remain exact.

A practical review method is to compare the output with the source sentence by sentence for high-impact material. Check names, dates, numbers, commitments, and any statement about a person or group. Then read the document as its intended audience would: Is the next action clear? Does the tone fit the relationship? Could a reader mistake a draft for an approved decision? Finally, check the destination and recipients. A correct message delivered to the wrong audience is still a problem.

AI may also make an output sound more certain or polished than the source. Preserve uncertainty where it matters. “The team is reviewing options” should not become “The team will implement the new process.” Good editing protects meaning before it improves style.

## Point 3 · Review and edit before use

Review is part of the task, not final polish. Check factual accuracy, completeness, dates, names, calculations, source citations, privacy, bias, tone, and whether the output obeys the request. Compare important claims with the original approved source. Ask whether a reader might mistake a suggestion for a confirmed decision. If a claim cannot be verified, remove it or mark it for review.

**Example:** A generated summary labels an idea “approved.” The meeting notes only show that it was discussed. A human editor changes the text to “proposed for review” and asks the owner for confirmation before the summary is shared.

**Apply it:** Use the [daily checklist](/resources/daily-checklist) as a pre-use and pre-send gate. Keep a brief review log for the module work product.

Review in layers rather than trying to catch everything in one glance. First check whether the tool used only the intended source and followed the requested format. Next verify the facts most likely to affect a person, a deadline, a resource, or a decision. Then inspect for missing caveats, bias, privacy details, or invented authority. Finally edit for clarity and voice. If the document is consequential, invite the normal reviewer to examine it before release; AI assistance does not change the established approval chain.

When an error is found, decide whether it can be corrected safely or whether the output should be discarded and restarted. A typo in a low-risk internal draft may be easy to fix. A summary that attributes a statement to the wrong person may need to be regenerated from the source or reviewed through the applicable record-correction process. Staff should not quietly overwrite official records without following local procedure.

Keep a record proportionate to the task. The course exercise asks learners to note what they checked because it teaches a review habit. Real work documentation requirements should follow DWIHN policy. Do not create or retain extra copies of sensitive content just to prove that an AI tool was used.

## Point 4 · Make the daily AI checklist a habit

The handbook's eight checks are **classify, confirm, minimize, prompt, review, verify, decide, report**. Before using a tool, classify the information, confirm the exact permitted workflow, and limit the input. After the output arrives, inspect it, verify important facts, and make the accountable human decision. If the tool behaves unexpectedly or information may have gone to the wrong place, follow the incident process. Repeating the same order reduces accidental shortcuts.

**Example:** Before drafting an update, an employee checks the tier and approved tool; after drafting, they verify numbers and recipients. A suspicious unsupported claim is removed, and the original source stays available for reference.

**Apply it:** Place the checklist where you actually open a tool or send work. Do not treat a completed checklist as a substitute for current policy.

The checklist works best as a pause at two transitions: before information leaves its source, and before AI-assisted work reaches another person or system. The first pause protects data and confirms permission. The second protects accuracy and controls distribution. If the output will be reused later, such as a template or report, it may need another review when the source, audience, or policy changes.

Teams can make the habit easier by naming a reviewer for recurring work, keeping the current catalog link close to the prompt builder, and sharing approved templates through the usual document channel. Managers should watch for shortcuts caused by workload pressure—for example, staff skipping the source check because the draft “looks right.” The response is to adjust the workflow and allocate time for review, not to treat review as optional.

The final “report” step is not meant to create anxiety. Most routine tasks will not produce an incident. It reminds staff to act promptly if a tool unexpectedly exposes information, returns a concerning result, or behaves in a way that could affect work. Knowing the reporting route in advance reduces the temptation to hide or quietly repair a mistake.

## Key takeaways

- Build workflows around responsibilities, permissions, and final human review.
- Emails, summaries, and reports each have distinct accuracy checks.
- A polished draft is not ready to send until checked against its source.
- Repeated pre-use and post-output checks make safe behavior routine.

## Practical scenario and exercise

**Scenario:** Select one recurring task in your role that could benefit from an AI first draft. Use only a synthetic version of its input.

1. State the business objective, intended recipient, data tier, approved workflow, and stop condition.
2. Write a five-part prompt and produce or sketch the first draft.
3. Mark at least three checks: a source fact, a possible omission, and a tone or privacy issue.
4. Make the human edits and document what remains for a supervisor or owner to decide.
5. Submit the prompt, draft, review log, and final version as the Month 1 milestone.

**Worked walkthrough:** A manager’s weekly update contains three completed tasks, one delayed item, and two options still under discussion. The tool produces a polished paragraph that says all six items are “on track.” The manager returns to the notes, restores the delay, separates completed work from discussion, and labels the two options as undecided. Before sending, the manager checks the recipients and asks whether the update is approved for that audience. The final review log records the material correction and the source used; it does not need to preserve sensitive text beyond the organization’s normal recordkeeping rules.

**Knowledge check:** Who owns a sent AI-assisted email? What is different about reviewing a report and a meeting summary? When should you report a concern?

**Answer cues:** The employee or role responsible for the communication owns the final message. A report needs measure, calculation, denominator, and interpretation checks; a meeting summary needs decisions, attribution, caveats, and action checks. Report a suspected exposure or other concern promptly using the official route rather than attempting an informal cleanup.

**Role transfer:** During the next team check-in, discuss the workflow without sharing sensitive examples. Identify which step currently causes rework: locating an approved source, drafting, checking the output, or getting final sign-off. If AI is appropriate and permitted, design the assistance around that bottleneck. If it is not, another improvement—such as a clearer template or a better handoff—may solve the problem with less risk.

After a small workflow trial, ask whether it improved more than speed. Did staff make fewer corrections? Were handoffs clearer? Did the output preserve the right caveats? Was the review time realistic? If the answer is no, adjust the process or stop using the AI step. A useful improvement should be apparent to the people doing the work and should not depend on hiding extra review or follow-up time.

**Facilitator discussion:** Ask learners to bring a recurring task from their role, then redesign it so the human check is part of the normal workflow. Have the group identify where the task stops if the source is wrong, the tool is unavailable, or the output changes a commitment.

**Action plan:** Identify one safe pilot task and the person who will review it. Continue with [Month 2](/course/month-2) and [Module 6](/modules/module-06).
