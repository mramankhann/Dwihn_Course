# Module 10 · Knowledge Assessment & Certification

**Month 2: Advanced AI & Lumenore Mastery · Week 9 · [Session 12](/sessions/s12-certification)**

## Topic introduction

The final module connects the first nine modules into one workplace decision sequence: define the task, classify information, confirm the approved tool, prompt clearly, verify output, decide as a human, and report a concern. The handbook calls for live Q&A, an individual 20-question assessment, and a DWIHN AI Readiness Certificate issued by Netlink and DWIHN. The course provides practice and a proposed standard; the program owners must approve the actual administration and certificate rules.

**By the end:** show safe reasoning across modules, complete the assessment, and know which guidance to keep using after the course.

## Point 1 · Review all nine learning modules together

Review by following a real task rather than memorizing isolated definitions. Ask what AI can help with, which catalog entry applies, how the data is classified, what a five-part prompt should say, and how to check the result. Then connect specialist work: validate a Lumenore measure, question a Genesys suggestion, distinguish an observed trend from a cause, and know when governance or incident reporting is required.

**Example:** A synthetic case asks for a program summary. The learner identifies the data tier, refuses an unapproved upload, writes a bounded prompt for permitted aggregate data, verifies the reported rate, and explains who approves the final communication.

**Apply it:** Use the [daily checklist](/resources/daily-checklist) as a reasoning guide, then complete the [capstone case](/projects/capstone) without looking at a model answer.

Review each idea by asking what action it changes. AI fundamentals tell you to verify fluent output. Tool approval tells you where the task may happen. Classification describes the information’s handling needs. Prompt design makes the request bounded. Workflow review catches unsupported facts before they enter a record or communication. Analytics skills test whether a result answers the intended question. Governance tells you where to take uncertainty and incidents. This connected view is the practical competency the course is meant to build.

Learners should be able to explain the reasoning in their own words rather than repeat a slogan. For example, “classify, confirm, review” is only useful if a person can describe what happens when a file mixes public and member data, the catalog does not mention a feature, or the AI summary says a tentative action was completed. Use the capstone to surface these judgment points and give feedback while there is still time to practice.

Preparation should include returning to earlier work products. Compare the S4 reviewed draft, the Lumenore validation log, the Genesys summary correction, the decision brief, and the incident tabletop. Look for the same habits across different tasks: source checking, uncertainty, role boundaries, and a clear next action. This makes the review feel like a coherent program rather than ten unrelated lessons.

## Point 2 · Use live Q&A to resolve uncertainty

Group questions by tool approval, data safety, prompting, role workflows, analytics, call center use, and governance. The instructor can answer course concepts, but local permissions and policy exceptions need an authoritative owner. Capture unanswered questions with the proposed use case, relevant data, and contact assigned to follow up. Share the confirmed answer through the approved training channel so the group does not leave with competing guesses.

**Example:** Several learners ask whether a new summary feature may process internal meeting notes. The trainer records the exact feature and information type, checks for a current catalog entry, and routes an unresolved question to governance instead of giving a speculative yes.

**Apply it:** Bring one question from your module action plans. Mark whether its answer is **course concept**, **current DWIHN policy**, or **tenant configuration**.

Q&A is most productive when unanswered items are captured rather than settled by the most confident voice in the room. Classify questions into those that can be answered from course material, those that need an official DWIHN policy owner, and those that depend on tenant setup. For each unresolved item, record the exact question, who will follow up, and how learners will receive the confirmed answer. If the answer is not available during the session, say so plainly.

Give the group a safe way to ask. Learners may be hesitant to describe uncertainty about a tool or a past mistake in front of colleagues. Invite general questions, allow written submission through an approved channel, and do not ask participants to paste member data into chat to illustrate a problem. If a real incident is disclosed, pause the classroom discussion and route the person to the official reporting channel.

Before closing, summarize which questions were resolved, which were assigned, and where the follow-up will appear. This creates a record of the program’s open decisions and prevents a provisional explanation from being repeated as official guidance.

## Point 3 · Complete the individual 20-question assessment

The handbook specifies an online, individual knowledge check. The [20 course questions](/assessment/final) use scenarios across tool choice, classification, prompt design, output review, Lumenore and Genesys concepts, objective decisions, and incident response. Answer independently, then use the feedback to revisit weak areas. The course also includes a practical case because recognizing a safe next step in a multiple-choice question does not always show that someone can perform it in a workflow.

**Example:** A question presents a mixed aggregate report with a member-identifiable note and asks for the safest next action. A strong answer identifies the sensitive element and stops until the approved handling path is confirmed; it does not rely on the report's title.

**Apply it:** Complete the assessment under the program's approved access and accommodation rules. Review the [practical rubric](/projects/capstone) before the final case.

Twenty questions can check coverage only if they sample the breadth of the course. A balanced test should include AI strengths and limits, exact tool approval, classification, prompt design, editing and verification, analytics definitions, call summaries and signals, trend interpretation, incident reporting, and human accountability. Use realistic situations so learners have to choose a safe next step. Avoid trivia about a product button that may differ across tenants.

Assessment conditions should be clear before the learner begins: time allowed, permitted resources, how to request an accommodation, what score is required, how feedback is provided, and what happens after an unsuccessful attempt. The handbook specifies an online individual assessment, while the site provides a practice question set. DWIHN and Netlink still need to approve the platform and administration details. The instructor key belongs in the private course source, not on the public learner page.

The practical case complements the quiz by asking the learner to show a process. Assess whether the person identifies the data, chooses an authorized path or stops, checks the source, corrects a material output error, and handles a possible exposure appropriately. Give feedback that identifies the missed reasoning step and a chance to practice it again.

## Point 4 · Understand the certificate and continuing responsibility

The proposal and handbook describe a **DWIHN AI Readiness Certificate** from Netlink and DWIHN. It should show that the learner completed the required program and demonstrated foundational safe-use knowledge. It is not permanent approval for every tool, feature, dataset, or future use case. The proposed course standard is 16/20 plus all critical safety steps on the practical case, but DWIHN and Netlink must approve the final threshold, attendance rule, retake path, accommodations, certificate wording, and issuer before certificates are awarded.

**Example:** A learner passes the course, then sees a newly introduced AI feature. They still consult the current catalog and data rule before using it for work.

**Apply it:** Keep the job aids, track policy changes, and ask for refresher guidance when tools or tasks change.

A certificate communicates completion under a defined program standard. It should not suggest that a learner is authorized to use every AI product or handle every data category. Access remains tied to the person’s role, the current catalog, the approved account, and the specific workflow. If a tool changes, a new use case emerges, or local policy is updated, staff must follow the current guidance regardless of when they completed training.

Before a certificate is issued, program owners should approve the issuer, wording, attendance requirement, knowledge score, practical criteria, accommodations, and retake process. The course’s proposed benchmark—16 correct answers out of 20 plus all critical safety actions in the practical case—is a training recommendation until the responsible owners adopt it. Avoid displaying a certificate design or announcing a pass rule as final before those details are confirmed.

Readiness continues after the ceremony. Encourage graduates to keep the job aids current, raise new tool questions early, and share approved workflows through established channels. A short refresher after major policy or product changes can reinforce safe habits and clarify new limits.

## Key takeaways

- Readiness means connecting safe tool choice, classification, prompting, verification, and accountability.
- Record unresolved Q&A and route it to the authoritative owner.
- The assessment tests reasoning; the practical case tests application.
- Certification documents a milestone and requires continued policy checks.

## Practical scenario and exercise

**Scenario:** Invent a **synthetic** work case that combines a data classification choice, a proposed AI task, a five-part prompt, an output error, and a final sharing decision.

1. Define the objective, information tier, and catalog approval needed.
2. Write the prompt with Role, Task, Context, Format, and Constraint.
3. Plant or identify one plausible AI error and explain how the human detects it.
4. State the final decision and who owns it; add a report step if the case includes a possible exposure.
5. Complete the [20-question assessment](/assessment/final) and [capstone](/projects/capstone) under the approved program rules.

**Worked walkthrough:** A final case describes a team that wants to summarize a synthetic report with aggregate program counts and one small cell. The learner notes the classification concern, checks whether the exact tool and export are approved, and pauses if the catalog is unclear. For a permitted aggregate exercise, they write a prompt that names the measure, period, audience, and format and tells the system not to infer a cause. The generated paragraph says the program change “resulted from staffing.” The learner removes that claim because no evidence supports it, verifies the count with the source, and prepares a limited report for the approved audience. If the scenario includes a mistaken share, they describe prompt reporting through the current internal procedure. The evaluation observes the reasoning at each step, not just the final paragraph.

**Knowledge check:** What does the certificate demonstrate? Which permissions must still be checked later? What happens to an unresolved policy question?

**Answer cues:** A certificate records completion and demonstrated foundational knowledge under the program’s approved criteria. It does not create blanket permission for future tools or data. An unresolved question is documented and sent to the proper owner; the learner does not guess. Instructors should explain any local scoring, accommodation, and retake decisions before administering the assessment.

**Role transfer:** At the end of the program, each learner should identify where they will find the current catalog, who owns questions for their workflow, and which job aid they will keep accessible. Managers can revisit these points after major tool or policy changes. This keeps readiness connected to current practice rather than to a certificate date or a saved prompt that may no longer fit the work.

For program administrators, keep a record of what the learner was assessed on, which rubric version applied, and how remediation was completed. This supports consistent certification without keeping unnecessary personal or sensitive material. Review assessment questions when tools, policies, or course objectives change; an item that once tested a stable principle may become misleading if it depends on a feature that has since changed.

**Facilitator discussion:** Ask each learner to explain a complete decision path using the synthetic final scenario. Listen for the source of the policy answer, the classification rationale, a clear human review, and an appropriate stop/report decision. Close by telling participants exactly where confirmed Q&A follow-ups and future policy updates will be shared.

**Action plan:** Keep the approved catalog, daily checklist, data desk card, and incident contact in your normal work resources. Return to the [course overview](/course/overview).
