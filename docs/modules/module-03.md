# Module 3 · Data Classification in Practice

**Month 1: AI Foundations & Safe Usage · Week 2 · [Session 2](/sessions/s02-tools-and-data)**

## Topic introduction

Classification is a decision made **before** choosing an AI destination. Real work often mixes public facts, internal notes, and sensitive details in one file. This module turns the handbook's four labels into a practical decision routine. The examples teach reasoning; DWIHN's current policy defines the authoritative tier and handling rule.

**By the end:** identify information that changes a document's sensitivity, use a memory aid, analyze mixed-data cases, and apply the desk card.

## Point 1 · Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted in context

Start with who may access the material, what harm disclosure could cause, and whether the content concerns a member, employee, vendor, or operational plan. **Public** suggests approved material meant for broad sharing. **Internal** covers work information that is not public. **Confidential** indicates tighter access. **Restricted** is the highest-control category in this training model and may include identifiable health details. A label alone does not tell you which AI tool is permitted; combine it with the catalog's use-specific rule.

**Example:** A published community resource page can be used to practice plain-language editing. An unpublished staffing note belongs in an authorized internal workflow. A member-specific care record demands explicit authorization and the highest applicable safeguards.

**Apply it:** Mark sensitive fields before copying a document. Attachments, screenshots, comments, metadata, and output exports may carry the same or greater risk than the visible paragraph.

Classification is about the **information and its context**, not the file extension or where it happens to be saved. A spreadsheet can contain public counts in one tab and confidential staffing notes in another. A screenshot may show a member name in a browser tab even when the chart itself is aggregated. A meeting transcript can include information that never appears in the formal minutes. Review the entire item that will enter or leave a tool, including headers, footers, comments, hidden columns, and attachments.

Ask who needs the information to perform the task, what could happen if someone else obtained it, and whether combining it with other details could reveal a person or sensitive operation. These questions help learners notice risk, but they do not replace DWIHN’s official classification definitions. When the same document mixes tiers, do not average the risk. Follow the handling rule for the most sensitive relevant content unless the authorized policy owner approves a narrower treatment.

One common mistake is to assume that a number is harmless because it contains no name. If a table has only one person in a category, the count itself may reveal a fact. Another mistake is to assume that everything inside the organization is simply “Internal.” Classification should guide handling in a consistent way across teams, not change depending on who is in a hurry.

## Point 2 · Use the wallet analogy as a memory cue

The handbook uses a wallet analogy to make the four labels memorable under time pressure. Imagine a Public flyer on a community table, Internal information kept inside the workplace, Confidential material protected like private financial papers, and Restricted information guarded with the strongest controls. The analogy is a reminder to pause, not a substitute for policy. It cannot determine whether a small aggregate cell is safe or which specific destination is approved.

**Example:** Before pasting text, a worker asks, “Would this belong on a public table, inside the office, in a protected folder, or in the most controlled system?” They then confirm the actual DWIHN tier and catalog entry.

**Apply it:** Use the quick question to interrupt an automatic paste. If two tiers seem possible, do not choose the lower one for convenience; seek the official rule.

Memory aids are valuable because employees often make handling decisions in the middle of another task. The analogy creates a brief pause: “Would I leave this on a public table, keep it inside the workplace, protect it like private papers, or place it under the strongest controls?” That pause can prevent an impulsive upload. It does not give a classification answer for every record, and it should never be used to argue that a sensitive item is safe because it “feels like” a lower category.

Try the analogy with a set of ordinary training examples: a publicly posted service description, a draft internal agenda, a staff evaluation note, and a synthetic member record. Ask learners to place each on the mental scale, then compare their reasoning with the organization’s official definitions. Differences between learners are useful teaching moments. They reveal where policy language or examples need clarification.

If a situation falls between two labels, document the uncertainty and ask the policy owner. Do not make the analogy more elaborate or invent an extra tier to solve the problem. The memory aid is successful when it sends people to the right reference at the right time.

## Point 3 · Work through mixed DWIHN-oriented data situations

A document's title may say “aggregate report” even when a footnote or appendix contains identifiable notes. Small counts, dates, ZIP codes, and uncommon circumstances can also make a summary sensitive. Identify the most sensitive relevant element, decide what the task actually needs, and separate or redact information **only when an authorized process permits it**. Check whether the reduced material can still reveal someone when combined with other facts.

**Example:** A synthetic report has an overall program rate, internal commentary, and one paragraph about a named member. The team cannot submit the full file as ordinary aggregate data. They isolate the public or aggregate question for a safe training exercise and seek guidance before processing the real mixed file.

**Apply it:** For every case, write: purpose → sensitive elements → provisional tier → official rule to check → permitted destination or stop decision.

Mixed data require two kinds of thinking. First, identify every element that could change the handling decision. Then ask whether the task can be completed using a smaller, permitted subset. That may mean working from aggregate figures instead of member-level notes, using a synthetic case, or asking an authorized data owner to prepare a training extract. Staff should not alter or de-identify a live file on their own unless the process explicitly permits it; a careless edit can damage the source or leave identifying details behind.

For ZIP-level analysis, check both volume and context. A ZIP code is not automatically identifying, but a very small cell, a rare service type, and a specific date can combine to point to an individual or reveal a sensitive pattern. A broad audience may also make a result more revealing than a restricted analytic view. A responsible reviewer considers the audience, purpose, cell size, available companion information, and applicable disclosure rules before presenting or exporting the chart.

During the exercise, ask learners to explain not only what they would remove but why the remainder still answers the work question. If the answer is “I think the name is the only identifier,” invite them to examine the other columns and the likely audience. The aim is careful reasoning, not a guess at a magic redaction threshold.

## Point 4 · Use the quick-reference desk card

The [desk card](/resources/data-desk-card) compresses a long policy decision into five checks: identify the tier, confirm the exact tool and feature, provide only permitted necessary context, verify the output and its destination, and know how to report a concern. It is most useful **at the moment of action**, before a file upload or copied prompt. Keep it visible until the steps become routine.

**Example:** A worker preparing a dashboard export checks the audience and small cells on the card before attaching the image to a meeting invitation. The card prompts a privacy review instead of an automatic share.

**Apply it:** Walk a peer through the card using a synthetic file. Note which answers depend on the current policy rather than on the course example.

Use the card at two moments. **Before use**, it helps identify the information, check the permitted tool, and decide what context is actually needed. **After the response**, it reminds the learner to verify claims, review the output audience, and report unexpected behavior. A card kept in a folder that no one opens does not change a workflow; place it near the point where staff access tools or prepare exports.

The card should be short enough to use and specific enough to direct a person to the right policy. It should not try to copy every exception into a tiny checklist. For example, the card can say “check the current DWIHN definition” and provide the location of the authoritative policy, rather than printing an outdated description of each data tier. The instructor should date or version the job aid and remove superseded copies through the approved document process.

Practice the card with a deliberately ambiguous synthetic scenario. Have a learner explain which step they can answer from the training material and which step requires the official catalog or a manager. This normalizes a safe pause and teaches people not to fill policy gaps with personal interpretation.

## Key takeaways

- A data tier guides handling, access, tool choice, and sharing.
- The memory analogy starts a check; the official policy completes it.
- Mixed files and small groups may be more sensitive than their titles suggest.
- Use the desk card before input and again before distributing output.

## Practical scenario and exercise

**Scenario:** A synthetic file contains aggregate program statistics, internal commentary, and a short member-identifiable note.

1. Highlight every part that could change the classification.
2. Give a provisional tier for each part and explain the handling choice for the complete file.
3. Identify a limited aggregate question that could be practiced without the member note.
4. Use the desk card to choose an approved path or document why the task must stop.
5. Check whether any table cell or combination could identify a person.

**Worked walkthrough:** The file contains a table with totals by ZIP, a paragraph describing a staffing change, and one note about a member’s uncommon referral situation. Do not classify the file by its title or by the first table. Mark the member note as the highest-risk element under the training model, then check whether the ZIP totals are sufficiently broad for the planned audience. If a safe aggregate question can be answered without the note, ask the data owner whether an approved extract is available. Do not delete the paragraph and assume the remaining file is safe: the description, dates, or very small cells may still reveal more than intended. The decision record should state which information is included, which policy rule governs it, and where the output may be shared.

**Knowledge check:** Why can a mixed report require the highest tier? What can the wallet analogy tell you, and what can it not tell you?

**Answer cues:** One sensitive element can change how the whole file must be handled unless an approved separation process exists. The wallet analogy reminds a user to pause and consider increasing care; it does not assign an official DWIHN tier or approve a tool. Look for answers that mention attachments, small cells, audience, and combinations of details.

**Role transfer:** Think of one file type you routinely receive—an agenda, spreadsheet, call export, or report. Before the next time you use it, identify where sensitive details might hide and which policy or owner defines its tier. Do not bring a real file into class to demonstrate the issue. Describe the file type in general terms and write down the question you would ask if its label or permitted tool were unclear. This turns the lesson into a repeatable pause at the point where information enters a new system.

**Facilitator discussion:** Ask groups to compare their provisional classifications for the same mixed file. Have each group name one additional data element that could change the decision and one question they would send to the policy owner. Close by asking learners where the desk card will be accessible during their usual workflow.

**Action plan:** Keep the desk card near your normal work entry point and ask the policy owner about one ambiguous document type you handle. Continue with [Module 4](/modules/module-04).
