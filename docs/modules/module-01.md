# Module 1 · What AI Is — and Isn't

**Month 1: AI Foundations & Safe Usage · Week 1 · [Session 1](/sessions/s01-what-ai-is)**

## Topic introduction

AI can draft language, summarize supplied material, and help people notice patterns. It does not know whether a DWIHN record is complete, whether an output is permitted to share, or whether a recommendation is right for a member. In this module, treat AI as an assistant whose work must be checked by the person responsible for the task. The examples below are **synthetic training situations**.

**By the end:** name useful AI tasks, recognize unsafe uses, and describe where human judgment enters a DWIHN workflow.

## Point 1 · AI is a capable assistant, not magic or a person

An AI system generates an answer from its training and the information available in the current task. It can produce a polished sentence without confirming the underlying fact. It does not carry professional responsibility, know unwritten local policy, or independently verify that a name, date, decision, or action item is correct. Give it a bounded task, then compare its output with the trusted source. The employee decides what to keep, revise, or reject.

**Example:** A coordinator supplies permitted, synthetic meeting notes and asks for decisions, owners, and due dates. The draft names an owner for a task that the notes left open. The coordinator removes that owner, marks the assignment as unresolved, and checks with the meeting lead before distributing the summary.

**Apply it:** Ask, “What source will let me verify this result?” If no reliable source exists, treat the answer as a suggestion or question, not a fact.

It helps to separate **the person who does the work** from **the tool that helps shape it**. An AI assistant can sort notes, suggest wording, or find a pattern in material it has been given. It does not know what a team privately agreed, whether an instruction is current, or which professional standard applies unless that information is supplied—and even then, it may misread it. Responsibility stays with the staff member who chooses the task, checks the result, and decides what happens next.

Consider a set of meeting notes with the sentence “follow up with the provider after the review.” The system may turn that into “Jordan will call the provider on Friday.” Those details make a tidy action item, but neither the owner nor the date appears in the source. The right response is not to admire the formatting; it is to mark the owner and date as unassigned. A useful assistant can make missing information more visible when the prompt asks it to do so. It cannot make the missing information true.

The same distinction matters in care coordination. A staff member may ask a system to group approved notes under headings such as “member request,” “actions taken,” and “follow-up needed.” The staff member still compares each item with the record, considers whether anything important was omitted, and uses the established care process to decide the next action. The system organizes words; the professional interprets what those words mean for the work.

## Point 2 · Where AI helps: drafts, summaries, and analysis

AI works well as a first pass on repetitive language tasks: an email outline, a plain-language version of approved text, a meeting summary, or a list of possible questions about a dataset. For analysis, it can organize observations and suggest comparisons, but the measure definition, data quality, and calculation still need checking. A good task has a clear input, a defined audience, and a way to test the answer.

**Example:** A manager turns approved bullet notes into a weekly update with sections for progress, risks, decisions, and next steps. Before sending it, the manager checks every claimed accomplishment against the notes, corrects an overstated risk, and adds the actual decision owner.

**Apply it:** Separate **source facts** from **AI wording**. Save time on structure and drafting while keeping the original material available for review.

Drafting, summarization, and analysis are related but different tasks. In drafting, the source may be a small set of approved facts and the requested output is new wording. In summarization, the output should preserve the important meaning of a longer source while reducing length. In analysis, the user asks a question of data or text and expects the system to compare, group, or describe evidence. Each task has its own check: a draft needs factual and tone review; a summary needs a completeness check; an analysis needs definitions, calculations, and filters checked.

For instance, a meeting summary can be shorter than the minutes and still preserve every confirmed decision, caveat, owner, and due date. If it removes the caveat “pending privacy review,” it is not a good summary even if every remaining sentence is accurate. A plain-language rewrite can simplify a public service description, but it must retain eligibility conditions and contact details. An analysis may correctly calculate a rate from the wrong denominator. Knowing which kind of task you asked for helps you choose the right review.

Start with work that is repetitive, bounded, and easy to check. A blank-page draft for a routine internal update is usually easier to verify than a complex recommendation about a person. When the task involves a consequential choice, first ask whether AI should be involved at all and who is authorized to decide. Productivity is not the only measure of whether a task is suitable.

## Point 3 · Uses that are unsafe or outside AI's role

Do not use a consumer tool for member-identifiable information merely because it is convenient. Do not ask AI to make a care, eligibility, crisis, or policy decision on its own. A generated answer may omit context, invent a citation, present a biased interpretation, or expose information through an unapproved destination. If the data classification, tool permission, or human decision maker is unclear, stop and check the current DWIHN rule.

**Example:** A worker wants a public AI website to rewrite a member communication that includes a member's history. The worker does not paste the history. They check the approved workflow and, if no permitted path is established, draft or seek help through existing authorized channels.

**Apply it:** Before entering data, state the task, information tier, approved destination, and person accountable for the final action.

Unsafe use often begins with a harmless-sounding request—“clean this up,” “tell me what to do,” or “summarize this call”—before anyone has checked what the text contains. A prompt does not neutralize sensitive information. Changing a member’s name to initials, removing a record number, or describing an unusual event in detail may still leave the person identifiable. Nor does a tool become suitable for workplace data just because it produces good answers or is available on a personal phone.

There is another boundary: AI should not quietly become the decision maker. A system may suggest a response, rank options, or draft a message, but staff must follow the applicable procedure and use their professional role. If the system’s output conflicts with a policy, the policy and authorized human process govern. If the output raises a concern—such as a fabricated fact, discriminatory wording, or a recommendation unsupported by the source—stop and resolve the concern before use.

Before starting, try to describe the task without mentioning the product: “I need a first draft of an internal meeting recap from approved notes.” Then ask whether the information is permitted in the chosen environment and who will review the draft. If either answer is unclear, the useful next step is to ask the owner, not to test the boundary with live information.

## Point 4 · DWIHN-oriented daily applications

Potential applications include structuring approved meeting notes, drafting an internal status update, making a checklist from a procedure, or explaining a validated aggregate chart in plain language. These are **examples for training**, not statements that a particular tool or dataset is approved. The best use cases remove routine preparation work and make the employee's review easier. They should not hide uncertainty or create extra privacy risk.

**Example:** An operations team has synthetic meeting notes about appointment follow-up. An AI draft identifies two decisions, three open questions, and proposed next steps. The team checks the notes, distinguishes agreed actions from proposals, and assigns each real follow-up in its normal work system.

**Apply it:** Start with a low-risk task you already understand. Define what a correct answer would include before asking for a draft.

A practical use case is strongest when it has four things: a repeated task, an input that can be shared through an approved path, a result that can be compared with a trusted source, and a named person who owns the final work. These conditions make it possible to learn whether the assistance is actually helpful. A faster draft that adds correction work or creates a privacy risk has not improved the workflow.

One team might use synthetic meeting notes to practice identifying decisions and open questions. Another might ask for a plain-language version of a verified public notice. A program manager might request a neutral description of an aggregate trend, then compare it with the underlying count and metric definition. These are training examples, not blanket approvals for a particular DWIHN tool. For each real task, the catalog and local policy still determine the allowed destination and data.

When trying a workflow, keep a short record of the original task, source, prompt, major correction, and final reviewer. This does not need to become paperwork for every low-risk draft. It is a learning aid: if the same error appears repeatedly, the team can improve the prompt, the source material, or the review step instead of treating each mistake as an isolated surprise.

## Key takeaways

- AI helps produce a first draft or possible pattern; a person verifies and decides.
- Fluent language is not evidence. Check facts, omissions, and unsupported claims.
- Tool approval and data classification come before entering workplace information.
- Choose bounded tasks with a trusted source and a clear reviewer.

## Practical scenario and exercise

**Scenario:** You have synthetic notes from an internal program meeting. They contain a discussion, one confirmed decision, and several proposed actions. You want a concise summary for the meeting lead.

1. State the work objective and classify the information under the training model; explain what official rule you would check for real notes.
2. Identify the approved tool or explain why you must pause if permission is unknown.
3. Write a prompt for **decisions, open questions, owners, and due dates**. Tell the tool to mark missing details rather than invent them.
4. Review the draft against the notes. Highlight one unsupported claim and correct it.
5. Write one sentence describing the human decision that remains after AI assistance.

**Worked walkthrough:** The notes say: “Discussed extending drop-in hours. Elena will check staffing. No date selected.” A useful prompt asks for one section of confirmed decisions and another for open items. If the answer says “The team approved extended hours beginning next month,” compare it with the source: both approval and date are inventions. Correct the recap to “The team discussed extending drop-in hours; Elena will check staffing; no start date was selected.” The draft can still be helpful because it made the topic easier to read, but the staff member has to preserve the status of the conversation. In a real task, the user would also confirm the tool, data, and destination before entering the notes.

**Knowledge check:** What makes a confident AI sentence unreliable? What is a useful low-risk work task? When would you stop before submitting data?

**Answer cues:** Confidence is a property of the wording, not proof that the source supports it. A useful task is bounded, repeatable, and easy for a responsible person to verify. Stop when classification, tool approval, or the output’s intended use is unclear. Ask learners to explain the evidence they would check; accept different examples when the reasoning is sound.

**Facilitator discussion:** Ask learners to distinguish a “nice-sounding answer” from a “verified work product.” Invite two people to review the same synthetic summary and compare which details they checked. Close by asking each learner to name one type of work where human judgment must remain visible.

**Action plan:** Choose one routine task, name its source and reviewer, and record one safety question to confirm with the current DWIHN catalog or policy owner. Continue with [Module 2](/modules/module-02).
